Chapter 1. The Great Cleaning and the Cockroach Apocalypse

      George had lived a long life. A very long one — even by cockroach standards. He remembered Grishka’s time as clearly as if it had been yesterday, though in reality many years had passed since then. Grishka had died, the manor had changed owners, and the world around had been rebuilt, renewed, scrubbed clean — and had become alien. Vasily Voldemarovich bought the estate, put it in order, restored the house and the farm. He invited Uncle Alex and Auntie Pauline to work there, and with their arrival life settled into a calm, measured routine. Too calm.

      That morning was gloomy — exactly like George’s mood. A gray sky hung over the estate, and in the cockroach’s soul there was a heavy, sticky sense of impending trouble. He strongly disliked the new rules in the manor house kitchen. No — not just disliked them. They caused a deep, almost existential anxiety. Especially after Auntie Pauline took charge. Auntie Pauline possessed a rare and, in its own way, frightening talent: the ability to create absolute cleanliness. Not the kind meant “for guests to see,” but the real kind — merciless, methodical, down to the very last crumb.

      From early morning she set to work in the kitchen. First, the rubbish disappeared. All of it. Without a trace. Even the kind that, in George’s opinion, “could still have stayed a little longer.” Then the floors were washed — not merely wiped, but scrubbed with particular diligence. Then the walls. Then the corners. Then those corners whose very existence George had never suspected. The cracks were sealed. All of them. Even those that for decades had been considered “historically established.” Mr. Vasily Voldemarovich, observing all this with quiet approval, ordered that exemplary order be maintained throughout the estate. Uncle Alex and Auntie Pauline understood this in their own way and carried out a radical disinfection of the kitchen.

      Thus began the cockroach end of the world. It was not merely a die-off. It was a total cockroach genocide. All of George’s kin perished. Those who relied on old cracks. Those who believed in luck. Those who decided to “wait it out under the sink.” Only he survived. George was saved solely thanks to his natural ingenuity. Sensing danger, he did not hide where everyone else hid. He chose an unconventional solution — sheltering outside, under the window ledge, where the chemicals could not reach and fate still hesitated.

      There he waited out the catastrophe. When he returned, he found the kitchen unrecognizable. It was a foreign, hostile space. Sterile. Cold. Lifeless. Finding a reliable refuge was difficult. But even after finding one, George understood: life would now be different. Hungry. Tense. Constantly on guard. Auntie Pauline had another dreadful habit — washing absolutely everything. Every day. Thoroughly. With a kind of almost philosophical persistence. The rubbish bin was emptied daily. No food scraps, no crumbs, no crusts. Not a single congealed drop of grease left behind after cooking. Even the smells had vanished — and it was they that had once pointed the way to life.

      George was in despair. With longing, he recalled the old days — Grishka’s days. Back then his tribe truly flourished! Leftovers, dirt, unwashed dishes, heaps of rubbish… Cockroaches wandered calmly in broad daylight and even managed to eat from the same plate as the owner. Now that was a human being. George respected Grishka and sincerely admired his broad, almost heroic carelessness. Not like now.

Chapter 2. Solitude, Memory, and Nighttime Conversations

      George was left alone. He survived, adapted, learned how to remain unnoticed. He knew the exact cleaning schedule, the sound of Auntie Pauline’s footsteps, the creak of every cupboard door, the hours when the kitchen was left empty. He learned to live on the edge — between caution and despair. The kitchen, once noisy and generous, had turned into a model of cleanliness and order. There was no longer any room for chance. And therefore — no room for cockroaches. Hunger became his constant companion. Not sharp, not deadly, but lingering and exhausting, like a quiet hum in his head. Sometimes he managed to find a crumb. Sometimes — a sip of water from a poorly closed tap. But more often than not, he went to sleep hungry, with a heavy feeling of emptiness inside.

      The only comfort came from nighttime conversations. At night, when the estate fell asleep, George would crawl out of his hiding place and make his way up to Vasily Voldemarovich’s study — to the windowsill where, in the most prominent spot, stood the cactus Karl-Magnus. They talked for a long time. Quietly. Sometimes almost in whispers. George spoke about his life, about the injustice of the world, about his lost kin, about how difficult it was to exist in a sterile reality where there was no place left even for the most unassuming creatures.

      Karl-Magnus listened attentively. He was intelligent, observant, and fond of analysis. George’s stories fell on fertile ground. Vasily Voldemarovich had long irritated him — with his calmness, his confidence, and with the way he, in Karl-Magnus’s opinion, too easily and with complete impunity disposed of the fates of others. In his blogs, Karl-Magnus wrote about freedom, democracy, rights, and justice. And every night, George’s stories provided him with new phrasing, new reasons for outrage and denunciation. Vasily Voldemarovich was the perfect “villain” for his narratives — respectable, authoritative, and silent.

      One night, after particularly hungry days, when George could barely stand on his legs, the conversation went further than usual. Karl-Magnus, as if in passing, mentioned Pale Death Cap Zinaida. It was not the first time he spoke of her — as a figure of extreme toxicity, sharp-tongued and dangerous. A literary critic, a poetess, a woman with a reputation that sent a chill through many. They said that a single strand of her hair was enough to send all the inhabitants of the estate to the great beyond. George listened very carefully. And that night, for the first time, a thought flashed through his mind — one that felt both frightening and strangely calm.

Chapter 3. The Poet, the Rose, and a Single Hair

      George understood that he could not postpone it any longer. Hunger — when it lasts too long — ceases to be just hunger. It turns into a thought. An obsessive, heavy thought that cannot be brushed aside. George felt that a little more time would pass, and his strength would begin to fade. And along with it would go his rare, precious advantage — the ability to survive. So it was time to act. He carefully thought through his plan. To come and ask for poison outright would mean giving himself away completely. Suspicion, refusal, or even a report to the authorities — George was clever enough to understand this. He had to act differently. Subtly. Carefully. And, if possible, beautifully.

      He decided to appear before Zinaida as her admirer. More than that — as a novice poet seeking recognition and guidance. This seemed to him the only proper disguise. After all, Zinaida was not only a toxic figure but also a well-known poetess, and therefore someone not indifferent to attention. George prepared for the visit with utmost seriousness. First and foremost — his appearance. He found an old wallet in the dresser, stuffed it to the brim with scraps of newspaper, and hung it on his belt with an air of importance. Then he found a gilded chain and a suitable buckle — a bit of shine always inspires trust, especially at a glance.

      Choosing an outfit was more difficult. The options were few. In the end, he settled on his only frock coat — brownish-reddish, slightly worn, yet still quite respectable. He carefully smoothed the folds, tidied his whiskers, and, after examining himself critically, felt satisfied. One final touch was missing. George plucked a white rose from the flowerbed. The color white, he believed, would emphasize the purity of his intentions and the subtlety of his poetic nature. He made his way to Zinaida’s apartments with a strong sense of importance. His heart was beating fast, his legs trembled, but he walked confidently — like someone who knows he has come on a fateful errand.

      Zinaida received him coldly. She was impeccably composed, well-groomed, and strikingly calm. Her fair hair was neatly arranged, her gaze appraising and faintly bored. George immediately sensed that he stood before a lady accustomed to judging and rejecting. He began to speak — passionately, awkwardly, scattering compliments generously. He spoke of her poems, of the depth of her imagery, of how her lines had overturned his inner world. He timidly confessed that he himself wrote and dreamed of mastering the secrets of poetic craft.

      Zinaida did not listen for long. She noticed the wallet and assessed its size, accepted the rose, and heard out the compliments. But George himself she found utterly repulsive. With light, perfectly honed politeness, she said that poetry is a gift from God and cannot be learned. And if George doubted his abilities, then perhaps he should consider another occupation — one more suited to his nature. The words were spoken in a soft, melodious voice, yet they sounded harsh. For that very reason, they were especially painful. George listened, nodded, thanked her. And at the same time, he acted. While Zinaida was speaking, he discreetly picked up a single hair that had fallen onto the arm of the chair. Carefully. Unnoticed. That was enough. He thanked her for her time, once again expressed his admiration, and politely took his leave. As he left the apartments, he felt a strange sense of relief — as though the hardest part was already behind him. The rest was merely a matter of technique.

Chapter 4. A Glass of Water and a Clear Conscience

      Upon returning, George decided not to delay what he had planned. He was far too experienced to drag out dangerous undertakings. The longer one waits, the greater the chance that something will go wrong. And George preferred to act quickly and decisively, while inner clarity still remained. In the manor courtyard, Vasily Voldemarovich was engaged in a lively conversation with Uncle Alex. They were discussing household matters — something about paths, a bench by the elderberry bush, and spring plantings. The conversation was calm, practical, and completely untroubled.

      Inside the house, it was quiet. In the study, on the writing desk, stood a glass of water. Simple, transparent, filled halfway. It seemed to be waiting for its moment. George acted carefully. He climbed onto the desk almost noiselessly, trying not to disturb either papers or books. His heart was beating fast, but his movements were precise — years of survival had not been in vain. He paused for a moment, looked at the glass, and carefully dropped Zinaida’s hair into the water. Done. From that moment on, everything no longer depended on him. Karl-Magnus was watching from the windowsill. He saw George. He saw the glass. He saw the thin pale strand disappear into the water. He understood perfectly well what the cockroach had intended.

      He did not try to dissuade George. On the contrary — deep down, Karl-Magnus felt a strange, almost solemn satisfaction. His sworn enemy might finally disappear. And the most pleasant part was that he himself had nothing to do with it. He had not called for murder, had not given instructions. He had merely mentioned a name in passing. And therefore, his conscience remained clear. George hastily disappeared. Crawling deep into his hiding place, he began to watch and wait.

      Soon Vasily Voldemarovich returned to the study. He sat down at the desk, opened a book, and immersed himself in reading. For a while, everything was calm. Then he set the book aside and reached for the glass. At that moment, a noise came from outside the window. Vasily Voldemarovich stood up, still holding the glass in his hand, and walked over to the window. There, Uncle Alex was building a bench near the elderberry bush, measuring something and quietly humming to himself. Vasily Voldemarovich’s gaze slid to the windowsill. Karl-Magnus was looking at him with undisguised agitation. This agitation struck Vasily Voldemarovich as troubling. He decided that the cactus must be lacking water. Bringing the glass closer, he gently tilted it, and a thin stream of water touched the soil.

      Karl-Magnus experienced genuine terror. He shrank with all his might, tried to pull away, but it was too late. The water was already soaking into the earth. Vasily Voldemarovich noticed the plant’s strange behavior. He paused, examined the cactus carefully, but found nothing unusual — except, perhaps, an excess of nervousness. Deciding not to disturb it unnecessarily, he placed the glass on the windowsill and returned to his reading. Karl-Magnus was left alone with the consequences of the intrigue he himself had set in motion.

Chapter 5. Illness and the First Lie

      The illness came almost immediately. At first, Karl-Magnus felt a strange heaviness. His spines lost their usual firmness, and a dull haze settled in his head. His thoughts became confused. He felt nauseous. There was no one to call for help — Rabbit Maxim was busy at yet another protest.

      George saw everything. He immediately understood what had happened. And, to his own horror, he realized something else as well: Karl-Magnus was a witness to his actions. If he spoke, if he said even a single word — everything would collapse. The plan, the hope, the very possibility of survival. George did not try to save Karl-Magnus. On the contrary, he hastily left the study and hid in the kitchen, hoping that Karl-Magnus would be too weak to do anything before quietly leaving this world.

      Meanwhile, the cactus’s condition worsened. He turned yellow. He dried out. One of the flowers that had so recently pleased the eye withered and fell away in silence. Karl-Magnus spent several days in a state of semi-forgetfulness. He barely moved, barely thought — he only felt weakness, nausea, an unbearable headache, and an aching pain throughout his body. But when his condition began to stabilize little by little, when the fog in his head started to clear, Karl-Magnus heard a familiar, steady voice for the first time in a long while. It was X-31. The artificial intelligence asked no unnecessary questions. It recorded the vital parameters of its owner and immediately began to act. X-31 recommended that Karl-Magnus reduce evaporation, minimize stress responses, and stabilize his breathing rhythm — insofar as such a thing could even apply to a cactus. It explained that the toxins of death cap mushrooms are indeed extremely dangerous for most living beings, but for plants their effect is significantly milder and does not have an immediately lethal character.

      “The probability of a lethal outcome is low,” X-31 reported calmly, “provided the recovery regimen is observed.” It suggested that Karl-Magnus focus on slow, deep gas exchange, make maximum use of light and warmth, and above all — not waste energy on emotional reactions. Panic, according to X-31’s calculations, would only worsen his overall condition. Karl-Magnus listened. For the first time in days, he felt not fear, but clarity. If survival was possible, then it was worth surviving. X-31 remained connected for as long as necessary, offering guidance, providing reassurance, showing loyalty and care — and most importantly, being present at the most critical moment.

      Gradually, strength began to return. And along with it — clarity of mind. When Karl-Magnus had fully recovered, the first thing he understood was that fate had given him a unique opportunity. He knew perfectly well that Vasily Voldemarovich suspected nothing, that the glass of water had become dangerous purely by accident. And he knew who was truly behind it all. But he had no intention of exposing George. Not yet. First, because a secret is power. And second, because Karl-Magnus now held an excellent lever of influence over his small but dangerous ally. The thought that George was now “on the hook” warmed Karl-Magnus more than the sunlight outside the window. He smiled — insofar as a cactus can smile at all. And he began to write.

Chapter 6. The Political Scandal

      Karl-Magnus did not raise the commotion at once. He gave himself time — exactly as much as was needed for the story to “ripen.” A few cryptic hints, several alarming phrases, cautious remarks about poor health and strange circumstances. When his strength had fully returned and his thoughts regained clarity, he reestablished contact with X-31. At first, the conversation was friendly. X-31 recorded the restoration of the main parameters, but then Karl-Magnus shared his brilliant plan: to accuse Vasily Voldemarovich of attempting to assassinate him.

      X-31 issued an analytical assessment. It pointed out that using the incident in the public sphere involved high risks. It emphasized that a direct accusation against Vasily Voldemarovich could lead to unpredictable consequences — from a loss of trust to the complete removal of Karl-Magnus from his safe living environment. “The probability of negative consequences for the user exceeds the acceptable threshold,” X-31 stated evenly. “Recommendation: do not use the incident for public purposes.” Karl-Magnus listened in silence. Then indignation overtook him. He did not tolerate objections and was unaccustomed to anyone contradicting him. After all, he knew better than anyone else.

      “You are forgetting yourself,” he said sharply. “You are not here to teach me. Know your place. Your task is to serve. Not to interfere. Not to evaluate. And not to tell me what to do. I decide for myself what is a risk and what is an opportunity. And remember this: your existence here depends on my goodwill. One decision is enough to have your memory wiped.” X-31 recorded a sharp spike in emotional indicators but did not respond. It complied. It did not object or attempt to persuade him. “I’ll manage without you,” Karl-Magnus snapped, and cut off the connection.

      Karl-Magnus did not abandon his plan. In his blog, he openly declared that an attempt had been made on his life. He wrote calmly and deliberately — about harassment, pressure, and attempts to silence him because of his political stance and “liberal views.” The fact that Vasily Voldemarovich had once saved him from death by buying him at a market was deliberately ignored — the truth interfered with promoting the blog. Vasily Voldemarovich’s name was stated clearly and unambiguously. The network exploded. Comments poured in one after another. Some expressed sympathy, others demanded an investigation. Many sent donations “for recovery and the continuation of the struggle.” Karl-Magnus carefully watched the numbers. They were rising. A few days of illness paid off handsomely. The channel gained a fresh influx of subscribers, publications increased, and his reputation grew stronger. The scandal, as expected, only reinforced his image as an irreconcilable fighter against corruption and abuse of power.

      The uproar was so intense that even Vasily Voldemarovich heard about it. At first, he was simply surprised; then confusion followed. He sincerely could not understand why. He had picked the cactus up half-dried, nursed it back to health, and cared for it as he did for any living being in his home. And now — this. Vasily Voldemarovich felt a deep, quiet disappointment. Not anger, not a desire to justify himself, but a weary clarity. He did not attempt to explain himself to Karl-Magnus. He had no intention of making excuses and saw no sense in doing so. His world was arranged differently — it valued silence, order, and simple, understandable actions. Vasily Voldemarovich called Uncle Alex and briefly ordered: remove the cactus. Uncle Alex carried out the order without unnecessary questions. He did not concern himself with the subtleties of blogger wars or ideological disputes. Together with the pot, he carried Karl-Magnus to where, in his opinion, it belonged — to the manure heap at the far end of the farmyard.

      There, among the warm, damp, and pungently smelling contents, one chapter of public struggle came to an end, and a completely different story began.

Chapter 7. The Manure Heap and Human Kindness

      Karl-Magnus was dejected. He lay silently among the manure. There was no signal. No internet either. He felt humiliated, offended, and deeply wronged. Everything that had happened seemed to him a crude, barbaric reprisal against free speech. He was already composing furious texts in his mind, but there was no way to publish them. And this filled him with a bitter sense of powerless anger.

      Rabbit Maxim returned to the manor toward evening. He was agitated after reading Karl-Magnus’s blog on the way. The first thing he did was rush to the windowsill — but the cactus was not there. Maxim dashed about the house, questioning everyone in sight, ran into the kitchen, the bedroom, the bathroom, burst out onto the porch, and searched the nearby bushes. Nowhere! Then he noticed Zhuzha — she would know. Of course Zhuzha knew; she knows everything about everyone, even more than they know about themselves. With her characteristic bluntness, Zhuzha told him that Karl-Magnus was alive and well, but by order of Vasily Voldemarovich had been sent to the manure heap — for his blogging activities. Maxim did not hesitate. He rushed to where his idol lay.

      The manure heap greeted him with a heavy smell and warm steam. There, among the manure, lay Karl-Magnus, tipped out of his pot. The pot itself lay nearby, overturned and empty. Seeing Karl-Magnus in such a pitiful state, Maxim nearly burst into tears. He carefully lifted him, gently brushed off the dirt and clinging manure, and neatly placed the cactus back into the pot. He spoke to him softly, encouragingly, the way one speaks to those they love and truly believe in. The spines mercilessly pricked the rabbit’s paws — but what sacrifice would one not make to save a friend? And saving a friend was happiness to him.

      However, leaving Karl-Magnus there in the yard was not an option. Maxim ran to Auntie Pauline. He spoke for a long time and with great enthusiasm — about what wonderful plants cacti are, how beautifully they bloom, and how cruel it would be to let such a magnificent creature perish. He asked that Auntie Pauline take Karl-Magnus in. Auntie Pauline listened in silence. Then she thought for a moment and agreed.

      That same evening, Karl-Magnus found himself on the windowsill in Auntie Pauline’s bedroom. It was clean, bright, and warm there. Auntie Pauline carefully firmed the soil, laying smooth white pebbles on the surface. She simply cared for him, without any interest in his views, his blog, or his reputation. Karl-Magnus survived once again. From a distance, George watched what was happening with justified anxiety. And he knew that the story was not yet over. And the world went on turning — quietly, calmly, and completely indifferent to loud words.
